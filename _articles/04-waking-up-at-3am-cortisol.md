# Waking Up at 3am? What Research Says About Cortisol and Night Waking

You fall asleep fine, then you're wide awake at 3am with your mind racing. Online, the usual explanation is a "cortisol spike." That's partly right, but it leaves out the most useful part: some rise in cortisol during the second half of the night is normal, and it happens to everyone.

So the better question is why you wake and then can't get back to sleep. Below is what the research says.

*This article is for general information and isn't medical advice.*

## What cortisol normally does at night

Cortisol follows a daily rhythm. It's low around the time you fall asleep, starts climbing in the early hours of the morning and peaks shortly after you usually wake ([O'Byrne et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35128146/); [University of Bristol, 2025](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2025/january/waking-stress.html)). That climb helps prepare your body for the day. We explain the morning end of the curve in [why cortisol is high in the morning](/guides/why-is-cortisol-high-in-the-morning/).

So if you check the clock at 3am, your cortisol is probably already on its way up. That's the clock doing its job. A cortisol rise on its own doesn't explain why some people wake fully and stay awake while others sleep through it.

## Why you might wake at 3am

| Possible reason | What the research shows | How strong |
|---|---|---|
| Normal light sleep and brief waking | Sleep gets lighter in the second half of the night, and brief awakenings are normal. Most people don't remember them. Staying awake is the part that varies. | Well established |
| Alcohol in the evening | A review of sleep-lab studies found alcohol helps people fall asleep faster but increases sleep disruption in the second half of the night, at all doses studied ([Ebrahim et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). | Strong |
| Getting older | In a study of 143 healthy adults, age raised cortisol in the late evening and early night and shifted the daily peak earlier ([Roelfsema et al., 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28760748/)). An earlier rhythm can mean earlier waking. | Moderate |
| A mind and body on high alert | Sleep researchers describe chronic insomnia as hyperarousal: raised arousal in the nervous system, hormones and brain activity ([Riemann et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19481481/)). People with insomnia also show moderately higher cortisol on average, though studies vary ([Dressle et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35091194/)). | Strong for hyperarousal; moderate for cortisol |
| Caffeine later in the day | In a small trial of 12 people, caffeine six hours before bed cut measured sleep by more than an hour ([Drake et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/)). More in [coffee and cortisol](/guides/coffee-and-cortisol/). | Moderate (small study) |
| A medical cause | Sleep apnea, reflux, needing to urinate, pain, low mood, thyroid problems and some medicines can all wake you. These need a doctor to check. | Varies |

The pattern most people describe, waking around 3am with a racing mind, fits hyperarousal better than a single hormone spike. Cortisol may add to it, but the research doesn't show that a "3am spike" is the cause.

## How to lower cortisol before bed (and what may help with night waking)

People search for "how to reduce a cortisol spike at 3am." No habit has been shown to stop the normal night-time rise, and you wouldn't want it to. What may help is making the evening calmer and making it easier to get back to sleep.

- **Go easy on evening alcohol.** It's the cause with the clearest second-half-of-the-night evidence.
- **Keep caffeine to earlier in the day.** The Drake trial found effects even six hours before bed.
- **Dim the lights in the evening.** Room light before bed shortened the melatonin signal by about 90 minutes in a study of 116 adults ([Gooley et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21193540/)).
- **Get up if you can't get back to sleep.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests stimulus control for chronic insomnia, which means leaving the bed when you can't sleep and returning when sleepy, as part of an approach led by cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)).
- **Don't watch the clock.** Checking the time tends to add worry. This is common advice in insomnia care rather than something with its own trial.

## When to see a doctor

Talk to a doctor if:

- You wake early and can't get back to sleep several nights a week for weeks.
- You snore loudly, gasp or stop breathing in your sleep.
- You wake to urinate often, or with heartburn, pain or a racing heart.
- You feel low or anxious most days, or wake with dread.
- You've started a new medicine around the time the waking began.

Waking early with low mood can be a sign of depression, and a doctor can help.

## FAQ

**Why do I wake up at 3am every night?**
Light sleep and brief waking are normal in the second half of the night. Staying awake is more likely tied to stress, alcohol, caffeine, age or a medical cause than to cortisol alone.

**Does cortisol spike at night?**
Cortisol normally starts rising in the second half of the night and peaks around waking. That's the normal rhythm, not a spike.

**What is waking up at 3am a sign of?**
On its own, nothing specific. If it happens most nights, or comes with snoring, low mood or other symptoms, a doctor can help work out why.

## The short version

Cortisol rises in the second half of every night, so a "3am cortisol spike" is mostly normal physiology. Waking and staying awake is better explained by alcohol, an alert mind, age-related rhythm shifts, caffeine or a medical cause. Calmer evenings and better habits for getting back to sleep have the most behind them. If this keeps happening, it's worth talking to a doctor.
