# Wired but Tired: Why You're Exhausted but Can't Switch Off

You've been tired since mid-afternoon, but when you finally lie down your mind starts running. You feel heavy and alert at the same moment. People call this being wired but tired, or tired but wired. They mean the same thing.

The short answer is that it's usually two systems pulling against each other. Your body clock, with the cortisol rhythm that runs alongside it, sets when you feel awake and when you feel sleepy. Your stress response can keep you alert even when the clock says it's time to wind down. Cortisol is part of that story, but it isn't all of it, and some of the explanations you'll find online have much less research behind them than others.

Below, we go through each common explanation and rate how strong the evidence is.

*This article is for general information and isn't medical advice.*

## What "wired but tired" means

Wired but tired describes a mismatch. Your body feels drained, but your mind stays alert, often at the exact moment you want to sleep. It isn't a medical diagnosis. It's an everyday phrase for a feeling a lot of people recognize.

Sleep researchers have a closer term for it: hyperarousal. In a widely cited review, Riemann and colleagues describe chronic insomnia as a state of raised arousal across the nervous system, hormones, immune markers and brain activity, which shows up during the day as well as at night ([Riemann et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19481481/)). You don't need to have insomnia to feel wired but tired now and then. But that research is the best place to look for why tiredness and alertness can sit side by side.

## Your body clock and the cortisol curve

Cortisol follows a daily rhythm. It starts rising in the early hours, peaks shortly after you usually wake, and falls through the day to its lowest point around the time you go to sleep. A master clock in the brain drives this rhythm ([O'Byrne et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35128146/); [University of Bristol, 2025](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2025/january/waking-stress.html)). The NIH notes that cortisol production normally drops just after we fall asleep ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/cushings-syndrome)).

That morning rise is normal and useful. It helps you get going. We explain the shape of the curve in [why cortisol is high in the morning](/guides/why-is-cortisol-high-in-the-morning/).

Being wired but tired fits the picture when the evening half of the day doesn't wind down the way it should. Bright light, late caffeine, stress and an irregular schedule can all push against the clock. If you tend to wake in the small hours instead, see [waking up at 3am and cortisol](/guides/waking-up-at-3am-cortisol/).

## What the research says about each explanation

Most pages on this topic list every cause as equally certain. Here's how we read the evidence, from strongest to weakest.

| Explanation | What the research shows | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Your whole system stays "switched on" | Sleep researchers call this hyperarousal. A major review found that people with chronic insomnia show raised arousal in the nervous system, hormones and brain activity, during the day as well as at night ([Riemann et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19481481/)). | Strong |
| Bright light in the evening | In a study of 116 adults, room light before bed shortened the night-time melatonin window by about 90 minutes compared with dim light ([Gooley et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21193540/)). Melatonin is the hormone that signals night to your body. | Strong for melatonin; less direct for sleep itself |
| Cortisol stays higher at night | A 2022 meta-analysis of 20 studies found people with insomnia had moderately higher cortisol than good sleepers, but results varied a lot between studies ([Dressle et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35091194/)). | Moderate |
| Caffeine later in the day | In a small trial of 12 people, caffeine taken six hours before bed cut measured sleep by more than an hour, and the participants didn't seem to notice ([Drake et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/)). See [coffee and cortisol](/guides/coffee-and-cortisol/). | Moderate (small study) |
| A "dysregulated nervous system" | A popular term, not a diagnosis. It describes much the same thing as hyperarousal, in everyday words. We unpack it in [what a dysregulated nervous system means](/guides/dysregulated-nervous-system/). | Popular term |
| Stress showing up as "cortisol face" or "cortisol belly" | Cushing's syndrome is a medical condition caused by too much cortisol over a long time, most often from steroid medicines. It can cause a round face and weight gain, and a doctor diagnoses it with lab tests. The form where the body makes too much cortisol itself is rare, at about 40 to 70 people per million ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/cushings-syndrome)). Whether everyday stress changes how your face or body looks is much less clear. See [cortisol face](/guides/cortisol-face/) and [cortisol belly](/guides/cortisol-belly/). | Unclear for everyday stress |
| "Adrenal fatigue" | The Endocrine Society says no scientific proof supports it as a true medical condition ([Endocrine Society](https://www.endocrine.org/patient-engagement/endocrine-library/adrenal-fatigue)), and a systematic review found no evidence for it ([Cadegiani & Kater, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-016-0128-4)). | Not supported |

The best-supported explanation is a body and brain that stay alert when they should be powering down, with evening light and late caffeine making that easier to slip into. Cortisol does seem to play a part. The meta-analysis found a real but moderate difference, and it can't tell us whether higher cortisol causes poor sleep or comes from it. So the habits with the most behind them work on your daily rhythm and your evening wind-down, and cortisol follows that rhythm.

## What helps when you feel tired but wired?

There's no single switch, and nothing here is guaranteed to work for you. But a few habits have research behind them, and they work with your body clock.

**Dim the lights in the evening.** Room light before bed delayed and shortened the melatonin signal in the Gooley study. Lower, warmer lighting in the last hours of the evening is a simple change.

**Keep caffeine to earlier in the day.** Caffeine six hours before bed still cut sleep in the Drake trial, and people didn't notice. If you're wired at night, the timing of your last coffee is worth looking at.

**Keep your wake-up time steady.** Cortisol's daily peak is tied to when you wake. A regular wake time gives the clock a steady anchor. This is our reading of the rhythm research, not a tested rule.

**Get up if you can't sleep.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's guideline for chronic insomnia strongly recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). It also suggests stimulus control: going to bed only when sleepy and getting up if you can't sleep, so the bed stays linked with sleep ([Edinger et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)).

**Give your evening a wind-down.** A calmer last hour gives an alert system a chance to settle. For practical ways to do that, see [how to calm your nervous system](/guides/how-to-calm-your-nervous-system/).

## When to see a doctor

Feeling wired but tired now and then is common. Talk to a doctor if:

- Your sleep problems last for weeks or keep coming back.
- You snore loudly, gasp or stop breathing in your sleep, or someone has noticed this.
- You feel low, anxious or on edge most days.
- You notice changes in your face, weight or skin that you can't explain.
- You have a racing heart, heat intolerance, or tiredness that doesn't lift with rest.
- You're pregnant or taking medicines that might affect sleep.

These can have causes a doctor can test for. A self-check online can't rule them out.

## FAQ

**Why do I feel tired but wired at night?**
Usually because your body is tired but your alert systems haven't switched off. Research on hyperarousal suggests this alertness involves your nervous system, hormones and brain activity, and evening light and late caffeine can add to it.

**Is tired but wired a sign of high cortisol?**
Not necessarily. People with insomnia have moderately higher cortisol on average, but the studies vary, and the feeling has other causes. Only a doctor's tests can say whether your cortisol is truly high.

**Is adrenal fatigue the reason I feel wired but tired?**
The Endocrine Society says there's no scientific proof that adrenal fatigue is a real condition. If you're tired all the time, a doctor can check for conditions that do exist.

## The short version

Wired but tired usually means your body is worn out while your alert systems are still on. Cortisol plays a part, but the research points more strongly to overall alertness, evening light and caffeine timing. Habits that support your daily rhythm are a sensible place to start, and lasting problems are a reason to see a doctor.
