Everyday Stress vs High Cortisol: When to See a Doctor

Lists of "high cortisol symptoms" are everywhere: tiredness, anxiety, poor sleep, weight gain, brain fog. Almost everyone has some of those some of the time. That's the problem with the lists. They mix up the normal effects of a stressful life with a real medical condition that needs a doctor.

This article explains the difference. It isn't a self-diagnosis checklist, and only a doctor's tests can tell you whether your cortisol is high.

This article is for general information and isn't medical advice.

Everyday stress raises cortisol, briefly

Cortisol rises when you're under stress, ill, or short of sleep. That's normal, and it usually settles. Cortisol also changes through the day, peaking in the morning and falling at night, so a "high" reading depends on when it was taken (O'Byrne et al., 2021; University of Bristol, 2025; NIDDK). Feeling wired, tired and stretched thin is common, and it has many possible causes. We look at that pattern in wired but tired.

Medical high cortisol: Cushing's syndrome

When doctors talk about high cortisol as a medical problem, they usually mean Cushing's syndrome, in which the body is exposed to too much cortisol over a long time. The most common cause is long-term, high-dose steroid medicine. When the body makes too much cortisol by itself, it's rare, at about 40 to 70 people per million (NIDDK).

Signs of high cortisol that doctors look for

The NIH lists signs of Cushing's syndrome that include (NIDDK):

  • Weight gain and a rounder face
  • Extra fat at the base of the neck, or a fatty hump between the shoulders
  • Easy bruising and wide purple stretch marks
  • Weak muscles
  • In women, irregular or stopped periods and extra hair on the face or body

It lists high blood pressure and type 2 diabetes among the complications.

The difference from everyday stress is that these are physical changes that build up and persist, often several at once. Tiredness or anxiety on their own aren't signs of Cushing's.

Things that can look like high cortisol

Some conditions raise cortisol and cause Cushing-like changes without true Cushing's syndrome. Doctors call these "pseudo-Cushing's" states. Examples include heavy alcohol use, depression and severe obesity. When the underlying condition resolves, the Cushing-like features and high cortisol go away (Endotext; NIDDK). This is one reason doctors repeat tests and look at the whole picture.

How doctors check

The Endocrine Society's guideline recommends that doctors first rule out steroid medicines, then use a high-accuracy test such as late-night saliva, 24-hour urine or a dexamethasone suppression test. An abnormal result leads to a second test and a referral to an endocrinologist (Nieman et al., 2008). When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you notice:

  • Several of the physical signs above, especially if they're getting worse
  • Weight gain with high blood pressure or high blood sugar that's new or hard to control
  • Changes in your face or body while taking steroid medicines (don't stop them on your own)
  • Ongoing low mood, anxiety or heavy drinking. These matter in their own right, and they can affect cortisol

If you're struggling to cope or thinking about harming yourself, get help now: in the US call or text 988; in the UK or Ireland call Samaritans on 116 123; elsewhere, call your local emergency number or find a helpline at findahelpline.com.

FAQ

What are the symptoms of high cortisol?

In Cushing's syndrome, signs include a rounder face, fat between the shoulders, easy bruising, purple stretch marks and weak muscles, with high blood pressure and diabetes as possible complications. Tiredness and stress alone aren't signs of it.

What are the signs of high cortisol in a woman?

The main signs are the same in everyone. The NIH also lists irregular or stopped periods and extra hair on the face or body as symptoms in women. A doctor can check.

Can stress cause high cortisol?

Stress raises cortisol for a while, which is normal. Medical high cortisol that stays high is usually due to steroid medicines or, rarely, a condition in the body.

The short version

Everyday stress raises cortisol briefly. Medical high cortisol is different: it's rare, it causes lasting physical changes, and it needs a doctor's tests. If you notice several of those changes, see a doctor rather than relying on a symptom list.
