Normal Cortisol Levels: Why 'Normal' Depends on Time of Day

People often search for one normal cortisol number. There isn't one. A normal cortisol level depends on the time of day, the kind of sample (blood, saliva or urine), the lab, and things like medicines. That's why your lab report gives its own reference range, and why the right person to read it is your doctor.

This article is for general information and isn't medical advice.

Normal cortisol levels by time of day

Cortisol follows a daily rhythm. It starts rising in the early hours, peaks shortly after you usually wake and drops after you fall asleep (O'Byrne et al., 2021; University of Bristol, 2025; NIDDK). So a result that's normal at 8 a.m. could be high at midnight, and a normal midnight result would be low in the morning.

As one example, MedlinePlus gives 5 to 25 mcg/dL (138 to 690 nmol/L) for a blood sample taken at 8 a.m., and notes that ranges vary slightly between labs (MedlinePlus). Your own lab's range is the one that applies to you. We don't reproduce a full chart here, because numbers without the time, sample type and lab method are easy to misread.

Blood, saliva and urine measure different things

  • Blood measures total cortisol, most of which is bound to proteins (Nieman et al., 2008; El-Farhan et al., 2017).
  • Saliva reflects free, active cortisol (El-Farhan et al., 2017). Late-night saliva tests are one of the tests doctors use to check for Cushing's syndrome (Nieman et al., 2008).
  • 24-hour urine measures cortisol passed over a whole day (MedlinePlus).

Each has its own units and ranges, so you can't compare a saliva number with a blood number.

Normal cortisol level for a woman

There's no separate "normal for women" number that applies in every case. But some things matter more for women:

  • Estrogen. MedlinePlus lists estrogen among the medicines your provider may ask you to stop before a blood cortisol test, because it can affect results (MedlinePlus). That includes birth control pills that contain estrogen (Nieman et al., 2008).
  • Pregnancy. Hormones change a lot in pregnancy.

If either applies to you, tell whoever orders your test.

Normal cortisol levels by age

Cortisol's daily rhythm changes with age. In a study of 143 healthy adults, older age was linked with higher cortisol in the late evening and early night and a daily peak that came earlier, by about 24 minutes per decade (Roelfsema et al., 2017). Most labs don't publish separate adult ranges by age, so these shifts are something your doctor takes into account rather than a number to look up.

What can push a result up or down

  • Time of day the sample was taken
  • Stress, illness, poor sleep or intense exercise around the test
  • Steroid medicines, and estrogen
  • Pregnancy
  • Heavy alcohol use and depression, which can raise cortisol (Endotext)

A single result outside the range isn't a diagnosis. Doctors repeat tests and use specific ones depending on what they're checking.

When to see a doctor

Talk to a doctor if:

  • You have a cortisol result and aren't sure what it means
  • You have physical changes such as a rounder face, fat between the shoulders, thin skin, purple stretch marks or muscle weakness (MedlinePlus)
  • You have weakness, dizziness, weight loss or low blood pressure
  • You take steroid medicines and are having symptoms

FAQ

What is a normal cortisol level?

It depends on the time, the sample type and the lab. Use the reference range on your own lab report, and ask your doctor to interpret it.

What is a normal cortisol level for a woman?

There's no single number. Estrogen, including in some birth control pills, can affect blood cortisol results, and so can pregnancy, so tell your doctor if they apply.

Do normal cortisol levels change with age?

The daily rhythm shifts with age, with an earlier peak and higher night-time levels in older adults. Labs usually don't publish separate adult ranges by age.

The short version

There's no single normal cortisol level. "Normal" depends on the time of day, the test, the lab and things like medicines. Your lab's range and your doctor's reading matter more than any chart online. For how stress and tiredness fit together, see wired but tired.
